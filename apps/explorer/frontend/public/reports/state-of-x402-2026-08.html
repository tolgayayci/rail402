The State of x402 on Stellar

x402 settlement works on Stellar. An x402 economy does not exist yet: total mainnet volume, ever, is about $11, and testnet is mostly machines testing machines. This report measures that honestly from public ledger data anyone can re-derive, and reads from the numbers what the ecosystem needs next.

Why you couldn't see this before01

x402 turns HTTP 402 into a payment flow for software: a client requests a resource, the server answers with terms, the client signs a payment authorization, a facilitator settles it on-chain, and the resource is returned. The buyer is usually an agent paying per request, no account, no API key.

On the ledger, that settlement is deliberately unremarkable. The transaction's source is the facilitator's channel account, not the buyer. The fee is paid by a sponsor the buyer has never heard of. The buyer exists only inside a signed Soroban authorization entry, and nothing anywhere says "x402," what was bought, or from whom. Point a classic explorer at one and this is what you get:

The Rail402 explorer watches both Stellar networks and classifies settlements structurally, by the shape only an x402 payment has (a detached, buyer-signed authorization entry plus a sponsored fee), so it needs no allowlist and indexes every operator's traffic, not just ours: mainnet history back to the first observable x402 payment on March 6, 2026, and testnet-shaped settlements back to December 22, 2025, before the Stellar scheme was even merged upstream. A payment is attributed to a facilitator only when that facilitator's own /supported endpoint publishes the signer we see on-chain; everything else stays honestly unattributed. Every figure below is one curl away.

Mainnet

An economy of $11

02

The headline number is 2,607 payments. The honest numbers are smaller. Total value ever settled through x402 on Stellar mainnet is 11.095738 USDC and 0.4202041 XLM: about $11, across 5.5 months. That is not a slow start to an economy; measured plainly, it is the absence of one, and recording that baseline honestly is this report’s job. Decomposed by cadence:

March–April is the launch window. The Stellar exact scheme merged upstream January 29; the first observable mainnet settlement landed March 6 at 16:51 UTC (0.001 USDC; the buyer and seller are the same address, an operator's smoke test); @x402/stellar hit npm March 10. The 776 payments of those 2 months are mostly integration shakeout: the 2 biggest March sellers have exactly 1 buyer each, and one of them is the smoke-test self-pair.

May is one experiment. 1,599 payments, 61% of all mainnet history, of which 1,580 flow to a single seller from 3 buyers on a 29-second metronome with identical amounts, May 2–18. That is a stress test of the rails (a successful one; it settled), not commerce.

July onward is the real signal. Small, but structurally different: irregular timing, varied amounts, growing participant counts. Since July 1, non-metronomic traffic runs at ≈4 payments a day from 13 buyers to 8 sellers, and August has the most distinct buyers (10) of any month since April.

Who is actually getting paid

The seller table is the honest census of mainnet x402 commerce: one stress-test target, a handful of launch-window test endpoints, and a small set of genuinely live services led by agent402.tools: 105 payments since July 4 at a median gap of ~3.3 hours with high variance. That rhythm is a service being used, not monitored.

Attribution: Built on Stellar (OpenZeppelin) settled 97.8% of mainnet history, verified against the signers it publishes itself, and carried the network alone from day one. The new development: an unattributed operator whose signers no known facilitator publishes began settling in early August and now accounts for most of the last week's payments (including all 8 of the last 24 hours at snapshot time). The mainnet field is no longer one operator deep.

Testnet

One very loud robot, and the real wave underneath

03

On August 7, testnet went from ~35 payments a day to over 2,000. The lazy read is "activity exploded 50×." The honest read is better. One buyer→seller pair (unattributed, identical amounts, one payment every 30 seconds, day and night) accounts for 22,776 payments, 76% of the surge. Its measured daily output is 2,878 payments; a perfect 30-second metronome would produce 2,880. Someone is soak-testing an x402 facilitator, continuously, since August 7.

Strip that single pair out and the surge is still the most interesting thing to happen on Stellar x402 testnet: ~1,600 payments a day (versus ~35 before August) from a broad base. In the last 24 hours: 121 distinct buyers (95 new), 129 distinct sellers (102 new). In the 10 surge days: 745 buyers and 3,219 seller addresses touched. That breadth cannot be one script; it is many teams bringing x402 infrastructure online at once.

10 pairs are the volume; 4,191 pairs are the people

All-time testnet volume is radically concentrated: the soak bot alone is 44% of every payment ever indexed, and 10 buyer→seller pairs cover 78%. The other extreme is the part that matters for adoption: 4,191 pairs paid exactly once: thousands of developers running a flow one time to see it work. Testnet's job is to be a proving ground; measured honestly, it is doing that job at record scale right now.

Settlements by operator

Coverage is a floor, not a total, and differs by operator: x402.org's history is fully walked back to March; Built on Stellar's pre-index automated heartbeat (a 10-minute self-check running since December 2025) is deliberately not backfilled, so its count reflects recent traffic, which is notably diverse: 1,023 distinct buyers, more than any other operator, consistent with being the facilitator many new teams test against. Rail402's row includes our own nightly canaries and conformance runs; we measure ourselves with the same ruler.

Method

How we tell a robot from a customer

07

What counts as an x402 payment

A single Soroban transfer (or a known upto contract's settle) authorized by a detached, buyer-signed authorization entry, where the buyer is not the transaction's fee payer. Ordinary wallet transfers and DeFi inner transfers don't have this shape. No allowlist decides what is counted, unknown operators are counted too.

The cadence test

For every buyer→seller pair with 30+ payments we analyze the gaps between settlements. A pair whose gap spread is tight around a fixed interval (IQR ≤ 35% of the median) is labeled metronomic: the signature of a scheduler, not a customer. The 30-second soak bot scores a spread of 0.00; the May mainnet stress run 0.21; agent402.tools' organic traffic 3.78. It's a heuristic and we present it as one: it catches monitors and soak tests, and deliberately does not catch bursty load tests (like x402.org's April traffic, thousands of payments in day-long bursts from a few addresses, obviously development activity, honestly counted as "bursty"). Intent is not observable; shape is.

3 confidence tiers, never faked

Every payment is labeled rail402 (our own facilitator), verified-facilitator (the on-chain signer appears in that facilitator's own published /supported response), or x402-shaped (structurally x402, operator unknown). If a signer isn't published, the payment stays unattributed: striped, not painted.

Every number is a floor

Stellar RPC retains roughly a week of history; older payments are recovered by walking verified facilitators' signing accounts through Horizon and watching known mainnet accounts, so coverage differs by operator and unknown-operator traffic older than the retention window is not yet recovered. On mainnet the explorer watches the USDC and EURC asset contracts and reports that scope in the API. Testnet resets wipe the chain's own history; the explorer's records are epoch-keyed and survive. The exact API responses and the full per-pair analysis behind this report were captured on 2026-08-16 and archived; the topline figures on this page refresh from the live API when you load it.

Schemes

2 ways to pay a machine: exact and upto

04

x402 payments follow settlement schemes: agreed shapes for how an amount is authorized and settled. 2 matter on Stellar today, and they answer different questions. exact answers "the price is known, pay it." upto answers "the cost is unknown until the work is done, cap it." Both are live on Stellar, and both have on-ledger proof you can click.

exactlive · mainnet + testnet

A fixed price, known before the call. The seller's 402 response says what the resource costs; the buyer signs an authorization for exactly that amount to exactly that recipient; the facilitator verifies and settles it on-chain.

The flow
  1. Agent requests the resource, receives 402 with a price: "0.05 USDC"
  2. Agent signs a Soroban authorization for that amount, that seller, with a ledger expiry
  3. Facilitator verifies, sponsors the fee, settles; the resource is returned
Built for
  • Per-request API pricing: a data lookup for 0.001 USDC, which is what agent402.tools charges on mainnet today
  • Pay-per-article, per-image, per-inference at a posted price
  • One-shot tool calls where the cost is knowable up front
Status: specified upstream for Stellar; every mainnet payment was exact until the first 4 mainnet upto settlements — ours — landed on 2026-08-17. Live proof: the first mainnet settlement · a smart-account buyer paying 1 USDC on mainnet
uptolive · testnet + mainnet · built by rail402

Authorize a spending cap, settle only actual usage, exactly once. The scheme for metered work, where nobody knows the cost until the work is finished.

The flow
  1. 402 quotes a ceiling: "up to 0.20 USDC"
  2. Buyer authorizes the cap once, bounded by a ledger expiry
  3. Service meters usage; facilitator settles the actual figure (say 0.075), never more, never twice
Built for
  • LLM token billing: the cost exists only after generation finishes
  • Metered infrastructure: compute seconds, bandwidth, storage, credits
  • Agent budgets that compose with smart-account spending policies on-ledger
Why it needs a contract

Plain SEP-41 allowances cannot bind the recipient or guarantee single settlement, so on Stellar upto requires a settlement contract. 2 are deployed, one per network, and both are Rail402's: no Stellar upto spec existed upstream, so we authored the design, shipped the contract, and battle-tested it: partial settlement, over-cap refusal, and replay refusal all proven on-ledger.

Status: 43 settlements on testnet, plus the first 4 on mainnet in real USDC, from classic keypairs and smart contract accounts; over-cap and replay refused on-ledger in every run. Live proof: a mainnet settlement of 0.075 USDC against a 0.10 cap · the full upto feed, live JSON

What the data says

Settlement is solved. Discovery is not.

08

Read the whole ledger at once and the diagnosis is hard to avoid. The rails work: sub-cent USDC payments settle in seconds, fees sponsored, from classic keypairs and smart accounts alike; the May stress run pushed a payment through every 29 seconds for 2 weeks without breaking anything. What the rails do not have is anything for an agent to buy, or any way to find it. There is no Stellar-native catalog of paid services with more than a handful of entries, and until this month there was no way to even see the problem. A working payment rail with no discovery layer produces precisely this ledger: monitors, stress tests, one-time experiments, and $11.

The timing signal in the data is the August wave: hundreds of new buyer and seller addresses per day arriving on testnet ahead of the infrastructure they will need. Builders are showing up before the shops exist. The moment to ship the discovery layer is now, while the conventions are still being set.

That is the work Rail402 exists to do, and everything we ship is pointed at the gaps this report measures: an open, self-hostable facilitator settling both exact and upto on testnet; a Stellar-native Bazaar with automatic cataloging and measured natural-language search; an MCP server so agents can discover and pay from inside their runtime; the only live upto implementation on Stellar; and this explorer, so progress is measured instead of asserted.

Discovery, shown working. The Stellar-native catalog is small enough to count on one hand today; that is what this entire report explains. The machinery above it is real and live: natural-language search that answers in agent-ready terms, with the price, the derived asset identity (a scam issuer using the code "USDC" derives a different contract), the seller's trustline state, and usage earned only by settled payments. The identical search is an MCP tool, callable from inside an agent runtime next to pay_and_call and its mandatory spending cap.
